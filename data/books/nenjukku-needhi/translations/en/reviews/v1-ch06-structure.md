## Governing question

Does the English preserve Kalaignar's movement from the public anti-Hindi campaign into his own schoolboy political awakening, through bodily confrontation and self-judgment, then forward across thirty-six years before widening again into martyrdom, Anna's interpretation and all-India Hindu-Muslim / Congress-Muslim-League politics—without flattening the chapter into detached political history?

## Structural map

### 1. The Tamil Brigade is introduced through people, not abstraction

Pattukkottai Alagirisami leads a Tamil Brigade from Tiruchirappalli toward Madras, with Moovalur Ramamirtham Ammaiyar and Manavai Thirumalaisami named as important figures.

Function: begin the chapter with an embodied marching formation and recognizable personalities rather than a generic description of anti-Hindi agitation.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 2. Kalaignar identifies three kinds of oratorical attraction

Alagirisami is introduced as a whirlwind Self-Respect speaker, and Kalaignar distinguishes what drew him in three speakers: the force and precision of Periyar's arguments, the valour brimming in Alagiri's lines, and the beautiful Tamil of Anna.

Function: make the political movement also a school in rhetoric for the young narrator.

English: **PRESERVED WITHOUT COLLAPSING THE THREE VOICES**.

### 3. Alagiri becomes a physical and rhetorical figure

Kalaignar gives him the epithet Summer-Thunder Alagiri, a strapping body, an unbowed kingly gait and a simple life; the news of a Tamil Brigade under him marching to Madras is called revolutionary.

Function: turn leadership into a vivid public image before the march itself unfolds.

English: **PRESERVED**, including the clipped portrait fragments.

### 4. Moovalur and Manavai make the movement operational

Moovalur's social-service record, widow remarriages and responsibility for food along the route are followed by Manavai's incisive political writing, `Pen Dance`, and his role as a commander.

Function: show that the march depends on organization, provisioning, writing and command—not speech alone.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 5. The two-hundred-mile march meets obstruction and grows through it

The Brigade walks from Tiruchirappalli to Madras, receiving welcomes from anti-Hindi supporters while Congress supporters attack it with sticks and stones; despite this, it grows from town to town and continues with a heroic stride.

Function: convert distance and opposition into a narrative of expanding momentum.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 6. The shoe-festoon insult sets up a public confrontation

Congress men hang shoes as festoons to humiliate the Brigade. Local Tamil sympathizers move to tear them down, but Alagirisami stops them and begins speaking before the insulting display.

Function: transform provocation into a test of political discipline and rhetorical power.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 7. Alagiri's speech is staged as animal and weather force

The tiger growls, the lion roars, summer thunder rumbles; his speech runs beyond two hours.

Function: enact, rather than merely report, Kalaignar's memory of Alagiri's oratorical impact.

English: **PRESERVED AS THREE DISTINCT EXCLAMATORY BEATS**.

### 8. Sarcasm turns humiliation back on the offenders

Alagiri says they have come to wipe away the stain threatening Tamil and the Tamil people, then jokes that if the shoes had at least been spread on the ground, each marcher could have worn a pair.

Function: convert insult into wit and moral advantage before the crowd.

English: **PRESERVED AS DIRECT SPEECH**.

### 9. Crowd anger is restrained and the offenders reverse themselves

The crowd surges to cut down the festoon. Alagiri insists that those who tied it must untie it. The Congress men apologize, remove it themselves, offer tea and send the Brigade onward.

Function: complete the scene as political reversal: restraint, not retaliation, produces public victory.

English: **PRESERVED IN SEQUENCE**.

### 10. The Brigade's public campaign becomes the narrator's own awakening

After sowing anti-Hindi feeling through small villages, Kalaignar says he himself is an example of how students were drawn to the cause; he recalls his school-age interest in forming student associations and holding weekly meetings and speeches.

Function: pivot from observed public movement to autobiographical formation.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 11. Student organization becomes daily street action and song

In 1938 Kalaignar leads students through Tiruvarur every evening with a Tamil flag, a cart depicting Rajaji stabbing Tamil Mother with the dagger of compulsory Hindi, and a song written by Kalaignar and sung by the students.

Function: show the young narrator imitating the movement's public forms—procession, image, slogan and song—and becoming an organizer himself.

English: **PRESERVED**, including the two-line refrain and the Tamil-Mother/dagger image.

### 12. The Jagadeesan hunger-strike episode introduces irony and political judgment

Kalaignar recounts Jagadeesan's long fast, the reported desire for Periyar's green-stone ring, Periyar's declaration that he had no faith in fasting, and the students later smashing the fasting man's picture after the fast had already ended.

Function: interrupt heroic mobilization with a skeptical, ironic anecdote and show political judgment forming alongside activism.

English: **PRESERVED**, including the reportative/hearsay status of the ring anecdote.

### 13. The Hindi teacher sees the agitation and the public struggle enters the classroom

The teacher sees the procession; Kalaignar hands him the anti-Hindi leaflet; the teacher leaves silently, and the next day's class becomes the answer.

Function: move the political conflict from street demonstration into immediate personal authority and discipline.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 14. Fearlessness becomes an explicit argument about devotion and “frenzy”

Kalaignar recalls feeling no fear while facing the teacher with the Tamil flag and shouting anti-Hindi slogans. Tamil surges in his blood and breath. He anticipates that some may call it `வெறி`, then accepts the term if devotion itself is to be called frenzy.

Function: turn autobiographical feeling into a self-conscious defence of intense mother-tongue attachment.

English: **PRESERVED**, with the frenzy/devotion relation still explicit.

### 15. Political difference is subordinated to love of Tamil

N. V. Natarajan, though working for Congress, joins the Tamil-protection movement; Maraimalai Adigal and other scholars who opposed Self-Respect ideas join Periyar against Hindi.

Function: argue that mother-tongue attachment can override existing party and ideological divisions.

English: **PRESERVED AS AN ARGUMENT AND RHETORICAL QUESTION**, not reduced to a list of allies.

### 16. The narrator's revolutionary self-image is tested through classroom violence

Kalaignar calls giving the leaflet to his Hindi teacher revolutionary. The teacher writes Hindi on the blackboard, orders him to read, twists his ear and slaps him when he says he does not understand; the classroom spins.

Function: bring the abstract language conflict into the narrator's body.

English: **PRESERVED WITH SHORT DIALOGUE AND BODILY IMMEDIACY**.

### 17. “My heart rendered justice to me” converts conflict into self-judgment rather than revenge

Kalaignar decides that what he did in the procession was right and what the teacher did in response was right too. Although teachers expect him to organize a strike or agitation, he does not retaliate; the teacher stays away briefly, picketing continues at the school gates, and Kalaignar stops attending Hindi class.

Function: supply the chapter's crucial moral/autobiographical turn: political conviction does not automatically become personal vengeance.

English: **PRESERVED**, including the title-bearing heart/justice phrase and the refusal of retaliatory escalation.

### 18. Thirty-six years collapse adversaries into later coexistence

Kalaignar jumps forward: thirty-six years have passed since the slap, and about five years earlier the same former Hindi teacher had waited to take him to a homeopathy conference. The teacher is now head of a major homeopathy institution.

Function: let autobiographical time reframe an old political conflict without erasing it.

English: **PRESERVED WITH RELATIVE-TIME VANTAGE INTACT**.

### 19. Mature pride returns the narrator to the language war and its dead

Thinking that he “sprouted” from the language struggle that flared up thirty-six years earlier brings him a majestic stride and proud gaze. He then recalls Thalamuthu and Natarajan as two Tamil bulls who became corpses in prison, followed by Anna's cumulative statement that compulsory Hindi exposed collective feeling, sacrifice, truth, labour, imprisoned mothers and life-giving gems.

Function: move from personal pride to collective sacrifice, then let Anna interpret the struggle's historical meaning.

English: **PRESERVED WITHOUT REDUCING THE BULL / CORPSE OR GEM / LIGHT IMAGES TO ABSTRACT MARTYRDOM**.

### 20. The chapter widens from Tamil Nadu into all-India communal and Congress–League politics

While the language war continues in Tamil Nadu, Hindu-Muslim conflict spreads in the north; Bose faces Muslim opposition in East Bengal; Jinnah challenges Congress; Gandhiji calls it a declaration of war. The Muslim League identifies injustices in Congress-ruled provinces, condemns compulsory Hindi in Tamil Nadu schools, and Muslim League leaders join the anti-Hindi ranks. The chapter ends with the Tamil flag and green crescent flag flying together.

Function: close by widening the schoolboy's language struggle into a larger Indian political field, while returning that wider politics to a concrete Tamil Nadu alliance image.

English: **PRESERVED WITHOUT ENCYCLOPEDIC REORDERING**.

## Gate result

**PASS.**

The English preserves Kalaignar's full structural journey. The chapter starts with an organized public language campaign and with the speaking styles that formed the young narrator; that public campaign becomes his own student organizing, street procession and song, then enters his body through the classroom confrontation. Instead of closing on resentment, Kalaignar's heart renders judgment, the narrative leaps thirty-six years forward, and mature pride reconnects his own formation to Thalamuthu, Natarajan and Anna. Only then does the chapter widen again into Hindu-Muslim conflict, Bose, Jinnah, Gandhiji and Muslim League participation in the anti-Hindi ranks.

The result therefore remains autobiography conducted through political history, not a detached chronology of the anti-Hindi agitation.
