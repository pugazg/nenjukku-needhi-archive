## Structural map

### 1. Orthodox ritual childhood

Kalaignar begins with the rituals his parents performed for him: ear-piercing, `Vidyarambam`, repeated head-shaving vows, sandal paste and temple offerings.

Function: establish the intensely ritual environment of his childhood before the later rationalist/social-critical turn.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 2. Hair-offering anecdote becomes comic theft

The deferred temple hair offering is stored carefully in a pot. A thief, finding nothing else, mistakes it for a money-pot and steals it.

Function: ritual detail is not treated solemnly for long; Kalaignar turns it into family comedy.

English: **PRESERVED**, including the delayed punchline.

### 3. Music education exposes caste humiliation

The father arranges music lessons, but Kalaignar explains why he develops no taste for them: before socially important men he cannot wear a shirt, must remove his upper cloth, and cannot wear sandals.

Function: personal dislike is immediately recast as an experience of social hierarchy.

English: **PRESERVED**. T2 restored `bondage`, `cruelty` and the pointed `big men` register.

### 4. Personal experience becomes political theory

The childhood response expands into a general claim: only one who has walked on stones and thorns knows their `pleasure`; people who never leave the jewel-studded bed cannot understand rough roads. Upper-caste reform is perfume upon an unhealed wound; revolutionary voices from backward communities can heal by surgery; the kicked ball rises with greater force; the oppressed person sets out in fury to erase degradation.

Function: this is the chapter's first major widening from autobiography into political interpretation.

English: **PRESERVED WITHOUT COMPRESSION**. All concrete images remain in the same argumentative order.

### 5. Father redirects the child toward schooling

Muthuvel recognises his son's disposition, concentrates him on schooling and arranges a private tutor paid partly in milk and vegetables.

Function: returns from political generalisation to household biography.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 6. Schooling opens into performance

The child begins acting, converts the cowshed into a theatre, plays Arjuna and Krishna, imitates temple festivals, makes a clay deity and conducts a mock procession using a bronze Rishabha-vahana toy.

Function: shows the early dramatist emerging from precisely the religious-cultural world he will later critique.

English: **PRESERVED**. The tension is left intact rather than explained away.

### 7. A physical object unlocks memory

Writing in the present, Kalaignar remembers the old bronze toy and longs to find it and place it before his desk.

Function: the autobiography openly demonstrates how memory is triggered.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 8. Reflection on youth and memory

The toy-memory widens into a meditation on nostalgia: the aged husband sees the youthful wife in his mind; dry leaves of memory rise in the whirl of thought; exhilaration contains joy-mingled sorrow that art itself cannot fully express.

Function: prepares the long return journey not as travel description but as emotionally saturated recollection.

English: **PRESERVED**, including the full aged-couple analogy and the joy/sorrow double register.

### 9. Present-tense homecoming journey begins

`Dazed by just such a feeling`, Kalaignar travels toward Thirukkuvalai by car with comrades. The prose becomes spoken pointing: `இதோ` / `அதோ` — Here / There.

Function: memory is staged as a live guided tour.

English: **PRESERVED**. Repetition is not edited away.

### 10. Kachanam: school-route memory and mantra

He points out Kachanam, remembers the bus stop, walking miles, kara-boondi, night fear and the protective mantra taught by his father and Saravana Swamiyar. Then the older narrator says the belief has now been uprooted.

Function: childhood belief and later rationalist self coexist in the same remembered route.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 11. Karugudi: domestic memory becomes joke

He remembers walking to buy curd, drinking some on the way, and jokes that village ponds existed to make good the resulting shortage.

Function: comic relief inside the nostalgic journey.

English: **PRESERVED WITHOUT EXPLANATION**.

### 12. Pattani deity: superstition becomes rational explanation

He recalls fear of the deity's torch procession, acknowledges that people really saw lights, gives a natural explanation involving sulphurous gas/flames, notes that some still reject it, and admits that he too once believed and trembled.

Function: rationalism is presented as a transformation of his own earlier consciousness, not as detached ridicule of villagers.

English: **PRESERVED**. The contextual `நாள் கூட` → first-person sense remains disclosed in source notes.

### 13. Owl / ghost-death bridge

Fear of an owl becomes a rhetorical challenge to the explanation that frightened deaths were caused by ghosts.

Function: sharpens the superstition/rationalist contrast without leaving the remembered child's fear behind.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 14. Muneeswaran: belief, absence and satire

The deity once speaks through Veerappa Velar during epidemics; Velar is now gone, disease continues, and Muneeswaran no longer comes. Kalaignar ends with the joke that when the deity's own plight is difficult, how can he help others?

Function: rationalist satire delivered through village-personification humour.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 15. Angalamman: family devotion becomes chicken-curry joke

Appa's desire for chicken curry immediately brings arrangements for a temple puja.

Function: affectionate family humour and religious practice remain interwoven.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 16. Curse-soil turns back on the curser

Devotees throw earth while wishing destruction upon disliked families; sometimes the wind blows the earth back into their own eyes.

Function: a physical comic reversal carries the criticism.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 17. Ruined school becomes `temple of knowledge`

The next landmark is the collapsed school that `made me what I am`. Kalaignar remembers writing `Arinamothu Sindham` in sand, then contrasts carefree childhood with adult burdens and calls the school the place that made laughing little sparrows strong enough to fly in life's sky.

Function: the journey turns from religious temples toward the formative `temple of knowledge` and from childhood innocence toward adult responsibility.

English: **PRESERVED**. T2's minimal functional gloss for the formula does not displace Kalaignar's remembered wording.

### 18. Houses become a catalogue of vanished people

Relatives' houses lead to elders, musicians and loving people now gone. Families are compared to banyan trees uprooted by a storm.

Function: spatial memory becomes mortality/history.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 19. Named figures alternate affection and wit

Vaidyalinga Mama waits for a Garuda omen—now he himself has `flown away`; Vaithi Thatha works with his spade; Appa fishes; Kuppusami Thatha's keys determine whether temple gods can eat; Thillaiamma and Vairiyatha become the village's two Avvaiyars.

Function: the dead return as vivid gestures and jokes, not as a formal memorial list.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 20. Agraharam / post office preserves a living voice

The postmaster calls across the pond. T2 correctly resolves his dialectal `நோக்கு` as `for you` rather than `look`.

Function: remembered sound enters the landscape directly.

English: **PRESERVED**, without fabricating an English regional accent.

### 21. Childhood house deepens the elegy

The house where he crawled and played tells both happy and painful stories. Karugudi Vaidyar and Ratna Padayachi are remembered, followed by the question: where are they now?

Function: the tour reaches its emotional centre.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 22. Entering the village converts memory into present affection

Relatives and friends surround him, renew ties, smell the crown of his head affectionately and offer hospitality. Kalaignar thinks of his parents and weeps.

Function: the dead past and living community meet.

English: **PRESERVED**, including the culture-specific head-smelling gesture.

### 23. Blessing returns to Muthuvel and childhood identity

Villagers rain kisses upon him and bless the `son of Muthuvel`.

Function: reconnects Chapter 3 with the father-centred Chapter 2 while preparing the child's departure.

English: **PRESERVED**. T3 restores the rain-of-kisses image.

### 24. Abrupt bridge to Chapter 4

At twelve he leaves for Tiruvarur to study, expecting admission to Second Form. The headmaster says he cannot be admitted even to Fifth Standard.

Function: closes nostalgia with a setback and creates the narrative hinge into the next chapter.

English: **PRESERVED WITHOUT FORESHADOWING OR EXPLANATION**.
