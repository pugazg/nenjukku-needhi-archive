## Governing question

Does the English preserve Kalaignar's movement from student-federation politics and ideological unease into the formation of the Tamil Students' Association, then through movement-building and festival enthusiasm into financial crisis, theft, family grief, guilty humour and a second theft that hurts his wife—without splitting the chapter into unrelated "politics" and "family" episodes?

## Structural map

The khadi-shirted organizer holds the Federation election, makes Kalaignar secretary and S. P. Chidambaram treasurer. From that moment the narrator worries where the organization is taking them: Communist or Congress.

### 2. Congress/Communist pressure sharpens into refusal

Congress-Communist dominance rises inside the Federation. A Congress executive member proposes `தமிழ் வாழ்க! இந்தி வளர்க` as the Federation slogan; Communist-sympathetic students agree; Kalaignar refuses.

Function: convert ideological unease into an explicit political line he will not cross.

### 3. Sleepless night leads to dissolution and fee return

Kalaignar cannot sleep. Dawn comes. He goes to treasurer Chidambaram, takes the one hundred rupees in membership fees, declares that the Federation will be dissolved and begins returning the money. Only twenty-five rupees is taken back.

Function: turn ideological refusal into immediate organizational action with concrete money.

English: **PRESERVED IN CAUSAL ORDER**.

### 4. The same-day Tamil Students' Association absorbs the remaining ₹75

That very evening the Tamil Students' Association is formed. The seventy-five rupees remaining from members who did not reclaim their fees becomes the practical bridge into membership in the new body.

Function: show continuity between dissolution and creation rather than an abstract organizational rename.

English: **PRESERVED**, including the secure 100−25=75 arithmetic.

### 7. 1941 inaugural event leads to the 1942 annual festival

### 8. Bharathidasan's verse turns organization into movement energy

The festival receives Bharathidasan's song, beginning with Tiruvarur and the Tamil Nadu Tamil Students' Association and rising to the image of a host of Tamil lions setting out against the enemy.

Function: convert organizational history into public cultural and emotional mobilization.

English: **PRESERVED AS VERSE**, not flattened into prose.

### 9. Festival enthusiasm collapses into a financial crisis

The Tiruvarur festival is underway; the Kumbakonam Dravidian Students' Association conference is beginning; Anbazhagan and Mathiazhagan arrive and speak. But most invited guests fail to come, travel money is not returned, those who did come must still be sent home, donors delay payment and time runs out.

Function: turn movement excitement into a practical crisis created by organizing.

English: **PRESERVED AS AN ESCALATING CAUSAL CHAIN**.

### 10. The gold-chain memory leads through internal debate to theft

With no financial solution, Kalaignar decides he must steal. He remembers the gold chain his mother wants him to wear around his wrist, the broken link and the chain kept in her box. He enters the house, argues internally with himself, sees the open box and the chain curled like a baby snake, trembles—and steals it.

Function: move directly from political necessity into intimate moral self-accusation.

English: **PRESERVED**, including the wrist-chain materiality, internal questions and baby-snake image.

### 11. The Chettiar Bank pledge solves the speakers' immediate problem

Kalaignar takes Thennan, pledges the chain at the Chettiar Bank in Tiruvarur for fifty rupees, gets the money and sends the speakers back to their towns.

Function: make the theft materially useful rather than symbolic: the domestic object pays the public political cost.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 12. Mother's grief and curses generate guilty comic reasoning

Home erupts. Mother searches, curses the unknown thieves and weeps. Kalaignar watches her grief, wants to cry, yet consoles himself that everyone eventually turns to dust and that his mother did not specify a deadline in her curse. He even joins the search for the chain that is actually at the bank.

Function: hold guilt and comedy in the same autobiographical voice.

English: **PRESERVED WITHOUT MORALIZING OR FLATTENING THE JOKE**.

### 13. Years of searching end in debt, sale, food and cinema

For two or three years Mother keeps searching the house and yard. Interest grows on the pledged chain until it is sold; the debt is paid; the remaining four or five rupees is spent by Kalaignar and friends on a hotel meal and cinema.

Function: extend the consequences of the theft while retaining the narrator's uncomfortable self-irony and concrete economics.

English: **PRESERVED**, including the leftover-money detail.

### 14. A much later confession changes the mother's response

Mother repeatedly mourns his bare wrist. Much later Kalaignar tells her the truth; she calls him a mischievous boy, and he recognizes approval on her face.

Function: retrospectively transform the concealed theft into a family memory without erasing the earlier hurt.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 15. Kalaignar explicitly links the first theft to a second

The narrator states that once he stole in a way that hurt his mother's heart, and another time in a way that hurt his beloved wife's heart.

Function: announce that the chapter's first theft is not an isolated anecdote but the model for the next movement.

English: **PRESERVED AS AN EXPLICIT STRUCTURAL BRIDGE**.

### 16. Sanarthanam's public meeting creates another return-fare crisis

Kalaignar invites Comrade Sanarthanam and sends him the fare to come, but has no money to send him back. The organizers and those who showed off on stage have already gone home. Kalaignar asks Sanarthanam to stay another day while he tries and fails to raise money.

Function: deliberately repeat the political-finance pattern that produced the first theft.

English: **PRESERVED**.

### 17. The sandalwood bowl becomes the second emergency loan

Kalaignar takes the sandalwood bowl given to his wife as a wedding gift, the bowl in which she keeps turmeric and kumkum, and gets relative Jeyaseelan to borrow ten rupees against it.

Function: make the second theft materially parallel to the first while changing the injured domestic relationship from mother to wife.

English: **PRESERVED**, including the ten-rupee amount and the bowl's domestic use.

### 18. The chapter closes with the wife blamed and the narrator grieving inwardly

The public-meeting problem is solved, but everyone blames and scolds his wife for losing the sandalwood bowl. She can only cry and cannot answer. Kalaignar does not reveal the truth and ends by saying he weeps inwardly as he watches her suffer.

Function: close not on successful political logistics but on the emotional cost shifted onto someone he loves.

English: **PRESERVED AS THE FINAL MORAL PRESSURE OF THE CHAPTER**.

## Gate result

**PASS.**

The English preserves the chapter's distinctive architecture. The chapter does not move from politics to an unrelated family confession. Instead, political organizing repeatedly produces concrete financial obligations; those obligations drive Kalaignar into domestic theft; domestic objects then bear the material cost of public action.

The first half moves from ideological refusal to a new Tamil student organization and then to movement expansion, verse and festival enthusiasm. That upward movement is deliberately interrupted by financial failure. The gold-chain theft solves the public problem while creating private pain, and Kalaignar's guilty humour prevents the scene from turning into solemn self-exoneration.

The second theft then repeats the structure at smaller scale: a public meeting, an unpaid return fare, another household object, another emergency loan, and another loved person made to suffer without knowing the truth. The chapter's structure therefore depends on repetition with variation, ending on the wife's helpless grief rather than on the narrator's successful political improvisation.
